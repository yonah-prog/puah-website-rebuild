Our origins

How it began

In 1978, the first baby was born through in-vitro fertilization (IVF) — an extraordinary scientific breakthrough that raised major halachic questions. Rabbi Mordechai Eliyahu zt"l, then the Chief Rabbi of Israel, charged Rabbi Menachem Burstein with researching a halachic framework to assist the Jewish community.

Rabbi Burstein recognized that Jewish men and women needed a wide network of resources to understand their fertility options — including supervision in laboratories to protect against errors and mix-ups, and one-on-one counseling by compassionate, educated professionals who understand the medical, halachic, and emotional aspects of each unique case.

In 1980, from a desk in his humble living room, Rabbi Burstein established PUAH. With Hashem's helping hand and a dedicated team, it has flourished into a worldwide organization that has counseled more than 100,000 families through their infertility struggles.

Family Purity

Q. I had my IUD removed today and there was no bleeding. Am I a niddah?

A. IUD removal does not automatically render a woman a niddah. The halachic status may depend on how the IUD was removed, whether cervical dilation occurred, and whether any bleeding was observed. In many routine removals without bleeding, a woman may remain tehorah. Because individual circumstances can affect the ruling, it is advisable to confirm the details with a qualified halachic authority.

General

Q. I noticed a small amount of spotting and then received a positive pregnancy test. Does this make me a niddah?

A. B’shaa tova! Not all spotting renders a woman a niddah. The halachic status depends on factors such as the colour and amount of blood, where it was found, and the circumstances in which it appeared. Spotting found on certain surfaces may be treated differently under halacha. A qualified rabbi should review the details before a determination is made. It is also important to discuss bleeding during pregnancy with a healthcare professional.

How lab supervision works

Who are the PUAH supervisors?

Discreet, dedicated women who are expertly trained in the halacha of fertility treatments. Each signs a confidentiality agreement, so your privacy is fully protected.

Is supervision a halachic requirement?

Establishing yichus (parentage) is paramount. Out of concern for human error, many poskim rule that an outside observer must monitor the procedure and storage of genetic material. Notably, Rav Yosef Sholom Elyashiv zt"l told PUAH that even if he himself were the embryologist, outside supervision would still be required.

Which stages require supervision?

All lab work is overseen — including IUI (sperm wash-up and insemination), sperm freezing, fertility preservation and egg freezing, frozen embryo transfers, surgical sperm retrieval, and IVF (egg retrieval, embryo checks, biopsy for genetic testing, and embryo freeze).

Do all rabbinic authorities accept PUAH's supervision?

Yes. PUAH is renowned for its cooperation with rabbis and poskim across the entire rabbinic spectrum.

How much does it cost?

To ease the financial burden of fertility challenges, supervision is fully subsidized by PUAH.

How do I request supervision?

Use the request form below or call our nationwide number 646-809-1407. Supervision is available at participating labs nationwide, pending availability and halachic psak, with a 24-hour minimum request.

Plain-language guide

The fertility dictionary

Every appointment arrives with new vocabulary — and no one stops to explain it. Here is the language of fertility care in plain English, with the halachic terms standing right alongside it.

Written for couples, not clinicians. Nothing here replaces your doctor or your rav — it just helps you walk in knowing what the words mean.

Home / Services / Fertility Dictionary
Start here

Five ideas worth understanding first

Most of the jargon hangs off these five concepts. Get these, and the rest of the vocabulary starts to fall into place.

Ovarian reserve

Roughly, how many eggs are still in reserve. Two numbers estimate it — AMH (a blood test) and antral follicle count (an ultrasound). They speak to quantity, not quality, and they are not a verdict on whether you can conceive.

The ladder of treatment

Care usually escalates in steps: timed intercourse → ovulation induction → IUI → IVF. Where you start depends on diagnosis and age — plenty of couples skip rungs, and that is not a sign anything went wrong.

Your protocol

The specific recipe of medications, doses and timing your doctor chooses for one cycle. Protocols get adjusted between cycles constantly — a change is normal fine-tuning, not a setback.

Monitoring

The stretch of early-morning bloodwork and ultrasounds during an active cycle, often every day or two. It is how the clinic watches your follicles grow and decides when to trigger.

The halachic layer

Treatment intersects with taharat hamishpacha, sperm collection, lab handling and more. Every one of these has answers — but they are individual answers. A shaalah to a rav who knows this field is part of good care, not an obstacle to it.

Diagnosis & the basics

Infertility

Not conceiving after 12 months of trying — or 6 months if the woman is 35 or older. It is a clinical threshold for when to investigate, not a verdict.

Worth knowing: roughly 1 in 7 couples meet this definition.

Primary infertility

Difficulty achieving a pregnancy in someone who has never previously achieved a pregnancy. It may involve male factors, female factors, a combination of both, or unexplained causes.

Secondary infertility

Difficulty achieving another pregnancy after a previous pregnancy, whether or not that pregnancy resulted in a live birth. It can occur even when conception happened easily in the past, and may require a new fertility evaluation.

Unexplained infertility

Standard testing came back normal and no cause was identified. It means the tests did not find it — not that nothing is there, and not that nothing can be done.

Heard when: workup is complete and your doctor still recommends treatment.

Reproductive endocrinologistRE

An OB/GYN who completed an additional fellowship in fertility and hormones. The specialist who runs IUI and IVF cycles.

Also written: REI — Reproductive Endocrinology & Infertility.

Ovulation

The release of a mature egg from the ovary, usually about 14 days before the next period starts.

Follicular phase

The first half of the cycle — from the first day of your period until ovulation. This is when follicles grow.

Luteal phase

The second half — from ovulation until your next period. Typically 12–14 days, and where progesterone does its work.

Follicle

A small fluid-filled sac in the ovary, each holding one immature egg. Monitoring ultrasounds measure follicles in millimetres to track growth.

Heard when: “You have eight follicles, the lead is at 18.”

Polycystic ovary syndromePCOS / PMOS

Increasingly referred to as polycystic ovary and metabolic syndrome (PMOS). A hormonal and metabolic condition that may cause irregular ovulation, irregular cycles and difficulty conceiving, and can be associated with elevated androgens and insulin resistance. Many effective treatments are available to manage symptoms and improve the chances of pregnancy, depending on the individual’s circumstances.

Endometriosis

A condition in which tissue similar to the lining of the uterus grows outside the uterus. It can cause pelvic pain, painful periods and difficulty conceiving. Diagnosis may involve symptoms, medical evaluation, imaging and, in some cases, surgery. Many effective treatment options are available.

Diminished ovarian reserveDOR

Fewer remaining eggs than expected for your age. It affects how a cycle is planned — it does not by itself mean pregnancy is out of reach.

Premature ovarian insufficiencyPOI

Ovaries reducing function before age 40. Previously called premature ovarian failure, a name that was both inaccurate and unkind.

Male factor

Any sperm-related contribution to difficulty conceiving. Present in roughly 40–50% of cases — which is why testing both partners early is standard, not an accusation.

PUAH note: collection for testing has its own halachic guidance. Ask before the appointment, not after.

Azoospermia

No sperm found in the ejaculate. Often still treatable — sperm can frequently be retrieved surgically from the testicle.

Oligospermia

A lower-than-typical sperm count.

Asthenozoospermia

Reduced sperm motility — how well sperm move.

Teratozoospermia

A low proportion of normally shaped sperm. Morphology is one of three standard semen measures, alongside count and motility.

Varicocele

Enlarged veins in the scrotum that can raise temperature and affect sperm. One of the more commonly correctable male-factor findings.

Tubal factor

Blocked or damaged fallopian tubes preventing egg and sperm from meeting. IVF bypasses the tubes entirely.

Fibroids & polyps

Benign growths in or on the uterus. Whether they matter depends almost entirely on size and location — many need nothing done.

Asherman’s syndrome

Scar tissue inside the uterine cavity, usually following a procedure or infection. Often correctable with hysteroscopy.

Tests & monitoring

Anti-Müllerian hormoneAMH

A hormone measured by blood test that helps estimate ovarian reserve and predict how the ovaries may respond to fertility medications. AMH does not measure the exact number or quality of eggs remaining and cannot, by itself, determine a woman’s ability to conceive naturally.

Heard when: your very first workup. Try not to let one number carry your whole week.

Antral follicle countAFC

An ultrasound count of the small resting follicles visible at the start of a cycle. Read together with AMH.

Follicle-stimulating hormoneFSH

The hormone that drives follicles to grow. Measured on day 2–3 of the cycle; a high level can suggest the ovaries are working harder than expected.

Luteinizing hormoneLH

The hormone whose surge triggers ovulation. This is what at-home ovulation predictor kits detect.

EstradiolE2

The main estrogen, rising as follicles grow. Tracked at nearly every monitoring visit.

ProgesteroneP4

The hormone of the luteal phase, which prepares and holds the uterine lining. Often supplemented after IUI and IVF.

Prolactin & TSH

Two routine blood tests — a pituitary hormone and a thyroid marker. Both can quietly disrupt ovulation, and both are straightforward to treat.

Semen analysisSA

A lab assessment of count, motility and morphology. Usually repeated, since results vary considerably between samples.

PUAH note: we guide couples on halachically appropriate collection methods and work directly with labs. Call before you schedule.

HysterosalpingogramHSG

his-ter-o-SAL-ping-o-gram

An X-ray taken while dye is passed through the uterus and tubes, showing whether the tubes are open and the cavity is normally shaped.

Heard when: scheduled early in the workup, in the first half of the cycle.

Saline infusion sonogramSIS

An ultrasound done with saline in the uterus to reveal polyps, fibroids or scarring. Gentler than an HSG and focused on the cavity rather than the tubes.

Hysteroscopy

A thin camera passed through the cervix to look directly inside the uterus — and, when needed, to remove what it finds in the same sitting.

Laparoscopy

Keyhole surgery through the abdomen to see the outside of the uterus, tubes and ovaries. Sometimes used to diagnose or treat endometriosis, though not every case requires surgery.

Baseline

The scan and bloodwork at the start of a cycle, confirming the ovaries are quiet and it is safe to begin medication.

Monitoring

The repeated bloodwork and ultrasounds during an active cycle — often every one to three days, usually very early in the morning.

PUAH note: when monitoring falls on Shabbos or Yom Tov, our phlebotomy service keeps the cycle on schedule.

Trigger shot

An injection used to complete egg maturation and coordinate the timing of ovulation, commonly given before IUI or egg retrieval. The timing of the procedure depends on the medication used and the protocol set by your fertility specialist.

Heard when: the call comes in the evening with a precise time. Set an alarm.

Beta

BAY-tuh

A blood test measuring hCG to confirm pregnancy — more sensitive and far earlier than a home test. Usually repeated 48 hours later to see whether the number is rising appropriately.

Heard when: about 9–14 days after transfer or IUI. The gap before it has a well-earned nickname: the two-week wait.

Endometrial lining

The uterine lining, measured in millimetres before a transfer. A “triple stripe” pattern on ultrasound is the appearance clinicians hope to see.

Medications

Clomiphene citrateClomid

An oral tablet that prompts the body to produce more FSH and ovulate. Often the first medication tried.

LetrozoleFemara

An oral alternative to Clomid, frequently preferred in PCOS and often easier on the uterine lining.

Gonadotropins

gon-AD-oh-TROH-pins

Injectable fertility medications used to stimulate follicle development. Gonal-F and Follistim contain FSH only, while Menopur provides both FSH and LH activity. These medications are commonly used in IVF and some IUI cycles.

AntagonistCetrotide / Ganirelix

An injection added partway through stimulation to stop the body ovulating before retrieval. The backbone of the common “antagonist protocol.”

Agonist / Lupron

LOO-pron

A medication that first stimulates, then suppresses, the body's own hormones. Used in long protocols and sometimes as the trigger.

Human chorionic gonadotropinhCG

The pregnancy hormone. Used as a trigger shot before retrieval or IUI, and measured as the beta to confirm pregnancy.

Progesterone support

Medication given during certain fertility treatments to support the uterine lining and early pregnancy. It is commonly used in IVF and some other protocols, but is not necessary in every fertility cycle. The need for supplementation, dosage and duration depend on the patient’s medical circumstances.

PUAH note: progesterone can cause spotting. Whether a particular stain carries halachic weight is a question worth asking rather than assuming.

Birth control pills

Sometimes prescribed before an IVF cycle. Counterintuitive, but it quiets the ovaries and lets the clinic control the calendar.

Metformin

An insulin-sensitising medication often used in PCOS to help restore regular ovulation.

Stims

Shorthand for the stimulation phase — typically 8–12 days of injections to grow multiple follicles before retrieval.

Treatments & procedures

Timed intercourseTI

Using monitoring or ovulation kits to identify the fertile window. The first and least invasive step.

PUAH note: if ovulation consistently seems to fall before mikveh night, this may be halachic infertility. Ovulation timing needs to be confirmed and the circumstances evaluated, medically and halachically. See the halachic terms below.

Ovulation inductionOI

Using medication to produce ovulation in someone who ovulates irregularly or not at all.

Intrauterine inseminationIUI

Washed, concentrated sperm placed directly into the uterus around ovulation. A short office procedure, usually painless, with no anaesthesia.

PUAH note: collection, washing and lab handling all carry halachic considerations. Supervision is available.

In vitro fertilizationIVF

Eggs are retrieved, fertilised with sperm in the lab, grown for several days, and one embryo is transferred to the uterus. Remaining embryos can be frozen.

Egg retrievalER / OPU

A 15–20 minute procedure under light sedation in which fluid is drawn from each follicle and eggs are collected. Recovery is usually the same day.

Intracytoplasmic sperm injectionICSI

IK-see

A single sperm injected directly into a single egg, rather than leaving fertilisation to happen in the dish. Standard practice in male factor.

Fertilization report

The call the day after retrieval telling you how many eggs were mature and how many fertilised. Numbers drop at each stage — that attrition is expected, not a failure.

Embryo transferET

Placing an embryo into the uterus through a thin catheter. Quick, usually without sedation, and typically the shortest appointment of the whole cycle.

Fresh transfer

Transferring an embryo within the same cycle as the retrieval, typically on day 3 or day 5.

Frozen embryo transferFET

A previously frozen embryo is thawed and transferred into the uterus. Frozen transfers can achieve success rates comparable to fresh transfers and may offer advantages in certain medical situations. The best approach depends on the patient’s individual circumstances and treatment plan.

Freeze-all

Freezing every embryo and transferring none in that cycle — to allow genetic testing, to avoid OHSS, or to let the body recover first.

Natural vs. medicated FET

Two ways to prepare the lining for a frozen transfer: following your own ovulation, or controlling the timing with estrogen and progesterone.

Protocol

The full plan for one cycle — which medications, what doses, what order, which trigger. Expect it to be adjusted between cycles.

Mini-IVF / mild stimulation

Lower medication doses aiming for fewer, good-quality eggs. Gentler and cheaper per cycle, with fewer embryos per attempt.

Egg freezing

Preserving unfertilised eggs for possible future use, while embryo freezing preserves eggs that have already been fertilised. Each option has distinct medical and halachic considerations. Depending on the circumstances, egg freezing may avoid certain questions associated with creating and storing embryos.

PUAH note: a decision with real halachic and practical dimensions. Worth a conversation before, not after.

Surgical sperm retrievalTESE / TESA / micro-TESE / PESA / MESA

Procedures used to obtain sperm directly from the male reproductive tract when sperm cannot be obtained through ejaculation or is absent from the semen. TESE, TESA and micro-TESE retrieve sperm from the testicle, while PESA and MESA retrieve sperm from the epididymis. The appropriate procedure and likelihood of success depend on the underlying medical condition.

Ovarian hyperstimulation syndromeOHSS

An over-response to stimulation causing swollen ovaries, fluid retention and discomfort. Modern protocols make severe cases uncommon — but report bloating, rapid weight gain or breathlessness immediately.

Third-party reproduction

Using donor eggs, donor sperm or a gestational carrier. Each raises substantial halachic questions about lineage and status that differ sharply between poskim.

PUAH note: this is precisely the territory where a general answer is no answer. Speak with us early.

The lab, eggs & embryos

Oocyte

OH-oh-site

The medical word for an egg.

Mature vs. immatureMII

Only mature eggs can be fertilised. Not every egg retrieved will be mature, which is why the retrieval number and the fertilisation number differ.

Zygote

ZY-goat

A fertilised egg in its first day, before it begins dividing.

Cleavage stageDay 3

An embryo of roughly 6–8 cells, three days after fertilisation.

BlastocystDay 5/6

BLAST-oh-sist

A more developed embryo of 100-plus cells with two distinct parts. Most transfers and freezes now happen at this stage.

Embryo grading

A laboratory assessment of an embryo’s appearance and development, often written as a score such as 4AA or 3BB. Embryologists use grading to help identify embryos with favourable characteristics for transfer or freezing. Grading does not guarantee pregnancy, and embryos with lower grades can still result in healthy births.

Euploid

YOU-ployd

An embryo whose tested cells show the expected number of chromosomes. In PGT-A testing, a small sample of cells is analysed to assess chromosome number. A euploid result may help guide embryo selection, but it does not guarantee implantation, pregnancy or the birth of a healthy baby.

Aneuploid

an-YOU-ployd

An embryo with a chromosomal number outside the expected range. The single most common reason an embryo does not implant or a pregnancy is lost.

Mosaic

An embryo showing both normal and abnormal cells on testing. A genuinely nuanced category — many have resulted in healthy births, and it warrants a real conversation.

Vitrification

Flash-freezing so fast that no ice crystals form. The reason modern survival rates after thaw are so high.

Embryologist

The scientist who handles eggs, sperm and embryos in the lab. You rarely meet them; they do some of the most consequential work in the building.

Witnessing

The lab's own protocol of having a second person verify identity at each handling step.

PUAH note: our lab supervision adds an independent, PUAH-trained observer present for your material specifically — an extra set of eyes, not a replacement for the lab's.

Assisted hatching

Thinning the embryo's outer shell to help it emerge and implant. Used selectively, not routinely.

Cryopreservation storage

The long-term tanks where frozen eggs and embryos are kept, with annual fees and consent forms. Ask early what the paperwork commits you to.

Genetics & screening

Carrier screening

A genetic test that identifies whether you carry a gene variant associated with certain inherited conditions. For many recessive conditions, risk increases when both partners carry variants in the same gene. However, some conditions, including certain X-linked disorders, can present risks even when only one partner is a carrier. Genetic counselling can help couples understand their results and options.

Dor Yeshorim

dor yeh-sho-REEM

The anonymous, number-based screening programme widely used in Orthodox communities. Results are not disclosed to individuals; compatibility between two numbers is checked at shidduch stage.

Autosomal recessive

A condition that appears only when a child inherits the same gene variant from both parents. Two carriers have a 25% chance with each pregnancy.

Ashkenazi panel

Screening for conditions found at higher rates in Ashkenazi Jewish populations — Tay-Sachs, Canavan, familial dysautonomia, Gaucher, cystic fibrosis and others. Sephardic and Mizrachi panels differ and matter just as much.

Preimplantation genetic testingPGT-A / PGT-M / PGT-SR

Testing embryos before transfer — for chromosome count (A), a specific inherited condition (M), or a structural rearrangement (SR). Requires IVF and a biopsy of the embryo.

PUAH note: whether, and when, PGT is appropriate is a halachic question as much as a medical one.

BRCA

BRACK-uh

Gene variants raising breast and ovarian cancer risk, more common in Ashkenazi families. Relevant to fertility planning and timing, and increasingly screened for.

Pregnancy, loss & after

Two-week waitTWW

The stretch between transfer or IUI and the beta. Clinically uneventful; emotionally, often the hardest part of the cycle.

You are not imagining it. This is the week most couples tell us they needed someone to talk to.

Implantation

The embryo embedding into the uterine lining, roughly 6–10 days after fertilisation. Where most of what can go right or wrong actually happens.

Chemical pregnancy

A very early loss shortly after a positive test, often before anything is visible on ultrasound. It was a real pregnancy, and it is a real loss.

Ectopic pregnancy

ek-TOP-ik

A pregnancy that implants outside the uterine cavity, most commonly in a fallopian tube. It cannot develop normally and may become life-threatening if internal bleeding occurs. Symptoms may include abdominal or pelvic pain, vaginal bleeding, shoulder pain, dizziness or fainting. Severe pain, fainting or other concerning symptoms require immediate medical attention.

Blighted ovum

A gestational sac that develops without an embryo. Also called an anembryonic pregnancy.

Recurrent pregnancy lossRPL

Generally, two or more pregnancy losses, which do not need to be consecutive. Evaluation may include genetic, anatomical, hormonal and other medical factors to identify possible causes and guide future treatment.

Viability scan

The ultrasound at around 6–7 weeks looking for a heartbeat.

Graduation

Being discharged from the fertility clinic to a regular OB, usually between 8 and 10 weeks. Expected to feel purely joyful; frequently feels unmoored as well.

High-risk pregnancyMFM

Pregnancy needing extra monitoring, managed by a maternal-fetal medicine specialist.

PUAH note: our guidance continues through pregnancy — the halachic questions do not stop at the positive test.

Halachic terms

Taharat hamishpacha

ta-ha-RAT ha-mish-pa-CHA

Family purity — the laws governing marital intimacy around the menstrual cycle. The framework within which every fertility decision is made.

Niddah

NID-dah

A woman’s halachic status following certain types of uterine bleeding, during which marital intimacy and certain forms of physical contact between spouses are prohibited. The status continues until the required halachic process is completed: a valid hefsek taharah, seven clean days, and immersion in a kosher mikveh.

Hefsek taharah

hef-SEK ta-ha-RAH

A halachic examination performed to establish that menstrual bleeding has ended. When performed correctly and at the appropriate time, it allows a woman to begin counting the seven clean days starting the following halachic day. Timing and requirements should follow halachic guidance.

Shiva nekiyim

shi-VA ne-ki-YIM

The seven clean days counted after the hefsek taharah, before immersion in the mikveh.

Bedika

be-di-KAH

An internal examination with a white cloth, used to establish and confirm cleanliness during the count.

Ketem

KE-tem

A bloodstain found on clothing, linen or the body. Whether it renders a woman niddah depends on several factors, including its size, colour, where it was found and the circumstances in which it appeared. These details determine its halachic significance, which is why they are carefully evaluated rather than assumed.

Heard when: treatment causes spotting. This is among the most common calls to the PUAHline.

Mikveh

MIK-vah

The ritual bath; immersion ends niddah status and permits marital relations to resume.

Harchakot

har-cha-KOT

The specific practices of separation observed while a woman is niddah.

Halachic infertility

A situation in which a woman’s fertile window may occur before she is halachically permitted to immerse in the mikveh, making conception more difficult. It may involve medical factors, cycle timing, or questions concerning the application of halacha. Because ovulation timing and halachic circumstances vary, proper medical evaluation and individualised halachic guidance are essential. Solutions may include medical treatment or halachic guidance.

This is where PUAH began. If your cycle is short, raise it with us. Many couples in this situation conceive without any fertility treatment at all.

Veset

VE-set

The anticipated date of the next period, around which certain separations apply. Cycle tracking has halachic as well as medical significance.

Shaalah

sha-a-LAH

A halachic question put to a rav. In this field answers genuinely turn on the details — which is why “I heard that someone said” is never a substitute for asking.

Hashgacha / mashgiach

hash-ga-CHA / mash-GEE-ach

Specialised supervision of reproductive material and laboratory procedures, and the trained supervisor who provides it, to help prevent identification errors and protect halachic integrity. Many poskim require or strongly recommend independent supervision during assisted reproductive procedures. PUAH provides trained supervisors who follow established protocols, helping couples pursue treatment with confidence and in accordance with their halachic guidance.

PUAH note: supervision is free to families and arranged directly with your clinic.

Hotza’at zera

hotz-a-AT ZE-ra

The halachic framework governing sperm collection. There are accepted methods for testing and treatment — the question is which applies to you, and your rav or PUAH can direct you before the appointment.

Pru u’rvu

pru ur-VU

The Torah’s mitzvah of procreation, expressed in the words “Be fruitful and multiply” (Bereishis 1:28). Halacha defines the obligation and its fulfilment according to specific parameters, including the birth of children. For couples facing infertility, the extent of the obligation to pursue various treatments is a complex and individualised halachic question. PUAH helps couples navigate these decisions with sensitivity, recognising that fertility challenges are beyond a person’s control and do not diminish their worth or spiritual standing.

Page patterns

Most pages are built from these blocks, in roughly this order.

Hero

Page hero

Eyebrow, headline with an italic accent, a lead line, then one or two buttons. Soft teal and blush glows in the corners.

Split head

Section heading

Headline on the left, a short supporting paragraph on the right, aligned to the bottom.

Bento

Service or hub grid

One large photo tile, one tall feature tile, and smaller tinted tiles, so every row is full.

Stage cards

Tall photo cards

Three or four tall photo cards side by side, for pillars or ways to get involved.

Inset panel

Deep panel

For services, audiences or a closing call to action. White text, mint accents.

Banner

Photo banner

A full-width photo with copy on a left-side gradient. Used for story and legacy moments.

Audience list

List and photo

Large rows with arrows; hovering a row swaps the photo beside it.

Closing

Final call to action

Every service page ends with "Speak to an advisor" and a phone number, with "free and confidential" stated.

Photography

Photos do most of the work of making the site feel warm. Until real photography is in, placeholders use the same tinted gradient.

Do

  • Real people in natural light: couples, advisors, rabbanim, supervisors at work
  • Candid, quiet moments; hands, conversation, home settings
  • Modest dress appropriate to the community
  • Warm color grading that sits well on the Bone background
  • Model releases for every identifiable person

Don't

  • Babies or pregnancy bumps as the default image; many visitors are still waiting
  • Lab-coat stock photos, test tubes, or anything clinical and cold
  • Identifiable patients without written consent
  • Text baked into images
  • Heavy filters or high-contrast black-and-white

Voice & tone

Calm, knowledgeable, and kind. Speak like an experienced advisor on the phone, not a brochure.

Do

  • Plain English first; explain Hebrew and medical terms the first time
  • Say "confidential" and "free" near every way to reach us
  • Qualify halachic and medical statements: "depends on the details", "a rav should review"
  • Write for couples who may be anxious, grieving, or still waiting
  • Use transliterations consistently: hashgacha, mashgicha, taharat hamishpacha, PUAHline
  • Use sentence case and avoid exclamation marks

Don't

  • Give individual psak or medical advice on the site
  • Promise outcomes ("very treatable", "guaranteed")
  • Assume a pregnancy will follow; stories should not depend on one
  • Publish numbers, costs, hours or titles without confirming them with PUAH
  • Present a paraphrase as a direct quote, especially from gedolim

Accessibility

Many visitors are reading on a phone, late at night, in a stressful moment.

Contrast

Body text at least 4.5:1. Muted grey text is never lighter than #5b6973 on Bone.

Tap targets

Buttons and pills are at least 40px tall, with 8px between them.

Phone first

Check every page at 375px. No sideways scrolling; bento grids collapse to one column.

Real text

Phone numbers are tap-to-call links. Headings are real headings, never text in images.
